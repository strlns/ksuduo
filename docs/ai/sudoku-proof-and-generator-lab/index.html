01 / EQUIVALENCE

A different grid.
The same mathematics.

Before deleting clues, distinguish a change of coordinates from a change of information. The 17-clue proof uses both ideas—but for different jobs.

A small correction to the starting picture. Symmetry reduction chooses one completed grid from each equivalence class. It does not turn a puzzle into one with fewer clues. And we start from a completed Sudoku: a Latin square must also satisfy the 3×3 box constraints.

|Γ| = 9! × 6⁸ × 2 = 1,218,998,108,160

There are 6,670,903,752,021,072,936,960 completed 9×9 Sudoku grids before this reduction. These are transformations, not necessarily distinct images of one grid. If some transformations return a particular grid to itself, they form its stabilizer (its automorphism group). Its orbit has size |Γ| / |Stab(S)|. Consequently, dividing the total number of grids by |Γ| is not the exact orbit count.

Why the reduction is a proof, not a sampling shortcut

Let g be any allowed transformation. If T completes a puzzle P, then g(T) completes g(P). Applying g⁻¹ gives the inverse correspondence. Thus g induces a bijection of solution sets: it preserves the exact solution count and the number of clues.

In particular, “contains a uniquely determining 16-clue subset” is constant throughout an orbit. One representative covers the entire orbit. A canonical representative can be the lexicographically least 81-digit string in that orbit—minlex. Least string does not mean fewest clues.

Structurally, each row/column group is S₃ ≀ S₃: the outer permutation rearranges the three inner groups. Transposition exchanges the row and column factors. Thus Γ = (S₉ × (S₃ ≀ S₃) × (S₃ ≀ S₃)) ⋊ C₂. The semidirect product records that these operations do not all commute.

Burnside’s lemma counts orbits by averaging the number of fixed grids across transformations. The established count is 5,472,730,538 equivalence classes. The proof used a complete canonical catalogue, not random representatives. Paper §3 ↗

For a generator: presentation shuffles give variety within an orbit. They cannot create new structural families from a single template.

02 / LOCAL ALTERNATIVES

Ambiguity is waiting
under the clues.

A trade is a different valid filling of a selected set of cells, with everything outside held fixed. It can exist before any clue is erased; erasure merely stops the clues from distinguishing the alternatives.

2compatible completions

From a visible shape to a general definition

Fix a completed grid S. For any other completed grid T, define its difference support D(S,T) = {cells where S and T differ}. If a clue set C misses that support, T agrees with every clue. Therefore C cannot determine S uniquely.

S is the unique completion of C
⇔ C ∩ D(S,T) ≠ ∅ for every T ≠ S.

This equivalence is the bridge to hitting sets. A set U is unavoidable for S when clearing U from the full grid admits another completion. Every uniquely determining clue set must hit U. Inclusion-minimal unavoidable sets have no smaller unavoidable subset; larger sets can contain several smaller trades.

Local trade ≠ global symmetry

The rectangle exchange is valid because these particular digits occupy these particular cells. Applying that same four-cell exchange to an arbitrary Sudoku need not work. Global transformations preserve validity for every grid.

Not every obstruction is a rectangle

Trades may use more cells and more digits, and their supports may overlap. The entire ambiguity structure cannot be captured by a list of four-cell shapes.

How this connects to the empty-row argument

Two rows in one band can always be exchanged. Their 18 cells are an unavoidable set in every completed grid: erasing both rows leaves the swapped completion available. A clue in either row breaks that specific swap. Other alternatives may remain.

The four-cell example similarly needs one clue in its support. Hitting one trade blocks that alternative; uniqueness requires blocking all alternatives. A trade need not appear only at a minimal puzzle, and a minimal puzzle need not have exactly two completions after one clue is removed.

For a generator: a rejected deletion can reveal a reusable obstruction, rather than returning only “multiple solutions.”

Minimal is a boundary. Minimum is an optimization.

A minimal puzzle is unique and every remaining clue is essential. A minimum puzzle has as few clues as possible in the specified search space. A greedy deletion pass can stop at a larger minimal puzzle while smaller ones exist elsewhere.

The monotonicity that really is safe

C′ ⊆ C ⇒ Solutions(C) ⊆ Solutions(C′)

Deleting clues can only preserve or enlarge the solution set. If deleting clue c admits an alternative completion, deleting other clues cannot eliminate that alternative. So c remains essential on this descending branch. A complete pass that tries each clue once and rejects only nonunique removals ends at a minimal puzzle; repeating the same pass cannot help.

Restoration changes the story. Adding a clue may block the old alternative and make c removable. In the 6-clue example, going sideways—restore some cells, then remove others—can reach a 4-clue endpoint. In fact, for one fixed S, any two unique clue sets C₁ and C₂ connect safely via C₁ ∪ C₂: add the missing C₂ clues, then delete C₁-only clues. A small exchange budget need not find that path. Equivalently, every unique target is reachable by pure deletion if we remove only clues outside it; greedy choices restrict which target we eventually reach.

Why difficulty is a different axis

Exact uniqueness is a semantic property. A logical grade depends on a technique catalogue, its implementation, rule ordering and stopping conditions. A failure by a bounded deterministic grader is not a certificate that every descendant is unusable.

Nor does fewer clues imply a higher measured grade. A removed clue can change candidates, the available deduction sequence and the first technique encountered. Use exact uniqueness to prune impossible descendants; use grading to steer, accept or reject candidates according to the product’s desired solving experience.

A generator that stops at a clue goal is not necessarily minimal. A generator that rejects removals because its technique solver stalls has not proved minimality with respect to uniqueness.

Your branching intuition is right. The stronger model is a subset graph with a monotone uniqueness boundary and a separately measured difficulty landscape.

04 / AN EXHAUSTIVE PROOF

Rule out the branches.
Keep every possibility.

The 17-clue theorem is global: some standard Sudokus have 17 clues; none has 16 or fewer. It does not say every completed grid can be reduced to 17.

1

Cover every completed grid

Choose one canonical representative from each of the 5,472,730,538 symmetry classes. The existence of a 16-clue puzzle is invariant under the transformations in chapter 1.

2

Replace blind clue selection with necessary constraints

For each grid, collect small unavoidable sets. Each requires at least one retained clue. A 16-clue candidate must hit every collected set.

3

Enumerate without omissions

Search the hitting sets with branch pruning and duplicate prevention. An incomplete catalogue admits extra candidates, but cannot exclude a real unique puzzle.

4

Check surviving candidates exactly

A complete solver tests uniqueness. No 16-clue candidate succeeds. Any smaller unique puzzle could be padded to 16 with solution digits, so those are ruled out too.

Primary paper announced 2012; revised paper 2013. Their computation used about 800 processor-years. Read the primary paper ↗

The search logic, derived step by step

Blindly testing every 16-cell subset means approximately 3.4 × 10¹⁶ choices for each completed grid. Write xᵢ = 1 when cell i is retained. Every unavoidable set U gives a constraint Σᵢ∈ᵁ xᵢ ≥ 1, alongside the clue budget Σᵢ xᵢ = 16. With every difference support included, these constraints characterize uniqueness exactly. With only a subset, they are a safe relaxation.

Branch on a set that has not been hit

If U = {a, b, c} has no selected clue, every valid extension must choose at least one of a, b, c. Partition the search into: choose a; exclude a and choose b; exclude a and b and choose c. These cases are exhaustive and disjoint. An extension containing both a and b belongs to the first case, not both.

If every known set is hit before the budget is filled, all eligible ways to add the remaining clues must still be covered. Previously excluded cells must remain excluded in descendant branches. Otherwise the same final clue set can be generated repeatedly in different orders. This is the role of the paper’s “dead clue” bookkeeping.

Prune with a lower bound on clues still needed

If r pairwise-disjoint unavoidable sets remain unhit, at least r additional clues are needed. With only k slots left, r > k proves the branch impossible. Overlapping sets cannot be counted this way: one clue might hit several at once.

A degree-d unavoidable set strengthens this: fewer than d retained clues in that region cannot establish uniqueness, even if every cell outside is given. Unions of d disjoint ordinary unavoidable sets supply easy examples; stronger configurations also exist. At each branch, compare outstanding requirements with the remaining budget.

This is a mathematical lower bound, unlike “this cell looks unpromising.” Small unhit sets are often good branching choices because they offer fewer alternatives; choosing their order does not lose completeness if every branch is still explored.

Why bitsets and a partial catalogue matter

Represent a clue mask and each obstruction as bitsets. Intersection tests become word operations. Per-cell hit vectors record which constraints each clue satisfies, and recursive state tracks which constraints remain unhit. These speed the same mathematical search.

The paper’s practical catalogue concentrated on unavoidable sets with at most 12 cells. Passing that filter was not a uniqueness certificate: remaining candidates still went to a complete Sudoku solver. Our four-cell-only experiment shows the same distinction at a smaller scale.

The proof also needed checks on the computation itself: completeness and distinctness of canonical representatives, validity of detected obstructions, and correctness of the solver’s counterexamples. The authors describe tests against known 17-clue puzzles and verification of alternative completions in §8.3.

The computation was exhaustive over all grid classes and all unexcluded 16-clue candidates. The achievement was making that exhaustive search feasible—not inferring nonexistence from many unsuccessful random trials.

The reusable principle is “necessary constraints first, exact verification last.” A generator can borrow it without reproducing a global minimum-clue search.

What the source supports—and what remains a hypothesis

The important exploration idea is already present. Uniqueness-only digging can pass through states that the logical ceiling rejects, followed by restoration into a different clue configuration. Recommending that as a brand-new feature would miss the current code.

The exact checker has room for a focused experiment. It scans cells in fixed order and tests digits by rescanning units. Compare minimum-remaining-values selection and incremental masks, preserving complete search. Its API currently returns a solution or a failure enum, so nonuniqueness witnesses are discarded. With a nonzero iteration cap, budget exhaustion shares a failure result with no solution; default generation calls use the uncapped setting. Inspect the checker ↗

The checker also rejects calls with fewer than 17 clues by using the known lower bound. That is reasonable as an application precondition, but this API cannot be used unchanged to independently re-prove the 17-clue theorem.

Time limits are soft. Deadlines are checked between probes or attempts. An individual uncapped uniqueness search can run past the nominal deadline. Cancellation and an explicit unknown / budget exhausted result would improve predictability; unknown must never be accepted as unique.

Randomized construction is not uniform sampling. Shuffling candidate digits and returning the first successful completion does not assign equal probability to all grids. A presentation shuffle preserves its orbit, and rejection based on measured grade conditions the output further. This need not be a product defect, but variety and uniformity are different claims.

Read the existing experiments cautiously

The repository reports 9/16 Hard hits for its final minimal-digging/repair variant versus 6/16 for the baseline on one development seed set. That came with extra runtime; it was not an equal-time, held-out comparison. An Extra Hard checkpoint variant was rejected after 3/8 hits versus 4/8 baseline.

These observations explain the current design, but do not establish population-wide speed or difficulty gains. No repository benchmarks were rerun for this essay. Unique-digging notes ↗ · Extra Hard notes ↗

06 / A TESTABLE NEXT STEP

Make a failed deletion
teach the next search.

A promising extension is a witness-aware uniqueness checker: preserve the alternative completion and turn it into a reusable constraint. This is a proposal, not a measured improvement to ksuduo.

THE CORE IDEA

Learn the cells that distinguish two worlds.

For the known solution S, ask the exact solver for a completion T ≠ S that agrees with the proposed clues. If one exists, save D = {i : Sᵢ ≠ Tᵢ}. Every future unique puzzle for this same S must retain at least one clue in D.

learn D → require C ∩ D ≠ ∅ → guide the next probe

The witness need not be a minimal trade to be sound. A smaller support may be stronger, but finding the smallest one adds work. If a cached set A is contained in B, B’s ordinary hitting constraint is redundant and can be dropped.

Where this helps—and where it duplicates existing work

In a single irreversible uniqueness-only pass, a failed clue is already permanently locked by monotonicity, and ksuduo tries it only once. A witness cache adds little merely to rediscover that fact. Its potential value is across sibling states, restoration, exchanges, beam search, or repeated trajectories sharing the same completed grid.

A restored clue in D blocks the old witness and may unlock the original clue. Track coverage: zero clues in a cached D means reject; exactly one makes that clue essential under further deletion; two or more means this particular obstruction no longer forbids either individual deletion. These are sound deductions, not a uniqueness guarantee.

Cache entries belong to S. If starting from a different completed grid, discard them unless a known transformation maps the old grid and cell supports to the new one. Do not assume similarly shaped trades apply to unrelated grids.

A bounded generator architecture to test

  1. Keep the independent final certificate. Have the search checker distinguish unique, multiple with witness, invalid/no solution, and unknown because of a budget.
  2. Test the checker before changing search. Compare fixed-order scans with masks and MRV on identical uniqueness queries; measure both proof-of-uniqueness and finding-a-second-solution cases.
  3. Reuse one S across a few trajectories. Retain a small archive of distinct clue masks, cached witness supports, achieved grades and lower-tier stalls. Exact mask deduplication avoids equivalent histories without claiming full symmetry canonicalization.
  4. Use coverage to guide exchanges. Restore a known-solution clue that covers useful learned obstructions, then attempt removals. Keep random exploration alongside the heuristic; exact checks and grading decide acceptance.
  5. Retain the best verified intermediate. At a deadline, return a previously certified puzzle with its actual grade and clue count. Do not confuse an unfinished search with a negative mathematical answer.

What would count as an improvement?

Use held-out seeds and equal elapsed-time budgets. Compare current production, extra ordinary restarts, a faster checker alone, and the checker plus witness-guided exploration. Separate the effects so the witness idea does not take credit for a faster solver.

Record requested-grade hit rate, exact clue-goal hit rate, actual clue counts, median and tail latency, timeouts, solver nodes, cache hit rate and repeated states. Verify every output’s uniqueness independently. If structural variety matters, assess equivalent-grid duplicates explicitly; differing strings are not evidence of different orbits.

Predictability can mean guaranteed validity, bounded effort and honest achieved difficulty. Guaranteed exact grade, exact clue count and broad unbiased coverage are separate, harder promises.
